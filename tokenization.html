INTRO / 02 · TOKENS

Why do LLMs read tokens, not words?

The Overview said a model predicts the next word. That was a small simplification. What it really reads and writes are tokens: pieces of text that are often a word, but not always.

02 / TOKEN VS WORD

A token is not quite a word

For everyday English the two often line up, which is why “next word” is a fair first picture. These are the places where they come apart.

  • Common words are one token. Rare words are several. “cat” is a single token; “antidisestablishmentarianism” is cut into pieces the tokenizer does know. Nothing is ever “unknown”: any text can be built from smaller pieces. Try it ↑
  • The space belongs to the token. Most tokens start with a space: cat (with the space) and cat (without) are two different tokens with different IDs. That is how the model knows where words begin without a separate “space” symbol. Try it ↑
  • Capital letters change the token. hello, Hello and HELLO are unrelated IDs as far as the tokenizer is concerned. The model has to learn from examples that they mean the same thing. Try it ↑
  • Punctuation and numbers are tokens too. A full stop is a token. A long number is chopped into chunks of digits that have nothing to do with its value, which is one reason models can be shaky at arithmetic. Try it ↑
  • Other languages and emoji cost more. GPT-2's tokenizer was built mostly from English text, so an accented word, a Japanese sentence or an emoji takes several tokens for what looks like one or two characters. Newer models use tokenizers trained on many languages and do better. Try it ↑
  • The model does not see letters. At the start of a text “strawberry” is three chunks; after a space it is a single token. Either way the model receives IDs, not letters, so asking how many r’s the word contains is asking about something it never looked at directly. Try it ↑

Why it matters to you

Limits and prices are counted in tokens. How much a model can read at once (its context window) and what an API call costs are both measured in tokens, not words. A handy rule for English: 100 tokens is about 75 words, so a token is roughly three quarters of a word.

Text is written one token at a time. When a chatbot's answer appears piece by piece, each piece is a token being predicted, exactly the loop from the Overview.

03 / WHY NOT WORDS OR LETTERS?

Three ways to cut the same text

A model needs a fixed list of pieces it can read and write. There are three obvious choices, and tokens are the one in the middle. The text is the one from the tokenizer above.

Letters0 PIECES

Tiny list, long text. A few hundred symbols cover everything, but every sentence becomes very long and each piece means almost nothing on its own.

Words0 PIECES

Short text, endless list. There are millions of words, names, typos and new coinages. Any word not on the list could not be read at all.

Tokens0 PIECES

The middle path. About 50,000 pieces: whole common words, plus smaller parts that can spell anything else.

04 / BYTE PAIR ENCODING

Where the pieces come from: Byte Pair Encoding

Nobody writes the list of tokens by hand. It is built from a large pile of text by one simple rule, repeated: find the two neighbouring pieces that appear together most often, and glue them into a new piece.

Building the vocabulary

Start with the smallest possible pieces: the 256 single bytes that all text is stored as. Count every pair of neighbours in the training text. The most frequent pair, say t and h, becomes a new token th. Count again, glue again. Early rules make common letter pairs; later ones make syllables, then whole words like the. GPT-2 stopped after 50,000 rules, which gives 256 + 50,000 + 1 special end-of-text token = 50,257 tokens.

Using it

To tokenize new text, the same rules are replayed in the order they were learned. A word starts as single bytes, and each rule that fits glues two neighbours together until no rule applies. Watch it happen with GPT-2's real rules:

Loading the tokenizer rules…

Each row is the word after one more rule. The label is the rule's number out of 50,000 (lower numbers were learned earlier, from more frequent pairs); the number on the right counts the pieces left. The orange pair is the one the next rule will glue.

A common word collapses to a single token in a few steps. A rare one runs out of matching rules early and stays in several pieces. That is the whole reason some words are one token and others are five.

What is real on this page, and what differs between models?

Everything here is computed by GPT-2's actual tokenizer: its 50,000 merge rules, published by OpenAI with the model, are loaded by this page and applied in your browser, so the tokens and IDs are the ones GPT-2 uses. No model is run; a tokenizer is only a set of rules.

Other models have their own tokenizers. They follow the same idea, usually with a bigger vocabulary (often 100,000 to 200,000 tokens) trained on more languages, so the same sentence gives different tokens and a different count in each model. Some use a close relative of Byte Pair Encoding with a different way of choosing what to glue. The “100 tokens ≈ 75 words” rule is for ordinary English; code, numbers and other languages use more tokens per word.
